Point-Mass Control — the Estimator Experiment

A continuous-state, continuous-action task: a point mass with position + velocity, driven by 2D force, rewarded for reaching the goal cheaply. A neural Gaussian policy (2-layer MLP) is trained by policy gradient. Four gradient estimators race on the same task, same seeds, same environment-step budget — the x-axis is environment steps, not wall-clock. (a)–(c) differ in how they reuse stored behaviour actions; (d) MSR-Replay instead corrects the state distribution with a learned density ratio, samples fresh actions from the current policy at replayed states, and takes its advantage from a fitted Q evaluation of the current policy. The scene runs in a single, strongly off-policy configuration — a large, stale buffer with a long reuse window — because that is the regime where replay bias and gradient variance actually bind; see Methods for the measured ranking. Methods — what is exact, what is estimated

Task MDP. State s = (x, y, vx, vy) ∈ ℝ⁴, start near (−0.75, −0.75) with small seeded noise, goal g = (0.75, 0.75). Action a ∈ ℝ² is a force, clipped to [−2, 2]². Dynamics (Δt = 0.1): v′ = 0.85 v + 0.1 a, x′ = x + 0.1 v′, with inelastic walls at ±1.1 (velocity zeroes on contact). Policy inputs scale velocity by 0.4. Reward r = −‖x − g‖₂ − 0.05‖a‖², plus +0.5 per step within 0.09 of the goal. Horizon H = 120 steps, γ = 0.99. simulated

Policy class. Gaussian πθ(a|s) = 𝒩(μθ(s), σ²I), μθ a 2-layer MLP (4→20→20→2, tanh), σ = 0.5 fixed, mean head 1.5·tanh-bounded. The value baseline V(s) = w·φ(s) is a learned linear function of φ(s) = (x, y, vx/2, vy/2, ‖x−g‖, 1), fitted by regression to sampled returns. Optimiser: Adam. Advantage per transition: Â = Gt − V(st), where Gt is the Monte-Carlo return-to-go of the episode that produced the transition (REINFORCE); the baseline term is re-evaluated under the current V whenever a transition is (re)used. Behaviour log-probability: each transition stores the raw Gaussian sample ã and its exact log-density log πb(ã|s); the environment executes the clipped action clip(ã, −2, 2) for dynamics and reward, while all score-function gradients and importance ratios are evaluated at the stored raw sample ã, so the stored logp is the true density of the quantity the gradients use. estimated

Gradient estimators — (a)–(c) use ĝ = mean over a batch of Â · ∇θ log πθ(a|s), differing only in data and correction; (d) changes the ingredients themselves (state weights, action source, advantage source):

(a) on-policy:  ĝ = (1/B) Σfresh Â ∇ log πθ(a|s)
(b) naive replay:  ĝ = (1/B) Σbuffer Â ∇ log πθ(a|s)  — no correction for πbehaviour ≠ πθ

In (d), Marginalized State-Ratio Replay, no stored action, stored return, or importance ratio is used at all. Three learned pieces replace them. State ratio: ŵ(s) ≈ dπ(s)/db(s) comes from a classifier Dη (MLP 4→20→20→1) trained to distinguish fresh on-policy states from buffer states; with balanced training batches the Bayes-optimal classifier gives ŵ = Dη/(1 − Dη), clipped at wmax = 5 and self-normalised per batch (w̃). Dη is refit every 16 replay updates — the same cadence as VRER's snapshot refresh — with 24 Adam steps (lr 0.01) on balanced 32 fresh + 32 buffer states. Fresh actions: at each replayed state, K = 8 actions are sampled from the current policy, so the action distribution is πθ by construction. Fitted advantage: Âψ(s,a) = Qψ(s,a) − Vψ(s), where Vψ(s) is the mean of Qψ over the same K fresh samples, and Qψ (MLP 6→32→32→1 over (s,a)) is maintained by fitted Q evaluation for the current policy: 2 minibatch TD steps (batch 32, Adam lr 0.005) per replay update, with expected-SARSA targets r + γ·(1/K′)Σk′ Qψ⁻(s′, a′k′), a′ ~ πθ(·|s′), K′ = 4, against a target network Qψ⁻ updated by Polyak averaging (τ = 0.01 per FQE step); Âψ is clamped to ±8, as for (a)–(c). This is why the buffer stores s′ and done alongside (s, a, r, G, log πb) — estimators (b) and (c) ignore the extra fields. MSR uses the same learning rate, batch size, replay-updates-per-episode, paired seeds, initial policy, and environment-step budget as the other replay estimators; its extra compute is B·K policy evaluations plus amortised FQE/classifier refits, at zero extra environment steps. The MSR card reports the ŵ effective sample size (Σw̃)²/Σw̃² of its replay batches and the mean sampled age, on the same footing as VRER's IS diagnostics. All MSR hyperparameters above were fixed from the design note before benchmarking.

Bias decomposition — which term each estimator corrects. Naive replay's bias splits into Bstate (buffer states are distributed as db, not dπ), Baction (score evaluated at behaviour actions, including baseline leakage: 𝔼a∼πb[V(s)∇log πθ(a|s)] ≠ 0), and Badv (the stored return-to-go estimates Qπb, not Qπθ). (b) Naive replay corrects none of the three. (c) VRER works on the variance axis — its IS weights address Baction and its control variate slashes the variance of doing so — and leaves Bstate + Badv in place. (d) MSR-Replay corrects Bstate via ŵ (up to classifier estimation error), makes Baction ≡ 0 by construction (fresh actions; the baseline identity 𝔼a∼πθ[V∇log πθ] = 0 holds again), and replaces Badv with the Q-approximation error of FQE — its bias no longer grows with buffer age or policy drift, but it has an approximation-error floor set by Qψ and Dη capacity, and Qψ is queried at fresh actions that may lie off the behaviour support (its named failure mode).

In (c), one importance weight w per transition — the current-policy ratio πθ/πb, with the log-ratio clamped symmetrically to ±ln 5 (w ∈ [0.2, 5]) — is shared by both batch terms, and both terms use the same batch and the same advantages Â. θ̃ is a snapshot policy refreshed every 16 replay updates, and μ̃ is the true full-buffer gradient under θ̃: the mean of w̃ Â ∇logπθ̃ over every window-eligible transition in the buffer, with w̃ = clip(πθ̃/πb, 0.2, 5) and the identical advantage definition. Because gcur and gref are evaluated on the same batch with the same weights, at a refresh (θ̃ = θ) their difference is exactly zero and ĝVR reduces to μ̃ — the control variate is exactly consistent, in the spirit (SVRG-style) of Variance Reduction Based Experience Replay for Policy Optimization. The VRER card reports the IS effective sample size (Σw)²/Σw² of its replay batches, so weight degeneracy is visible rather than hidden. Replay agents additionally perform 8 replay updates (at half the fresh-update learning rate) per episode; the on-policy agent does not — that difference is the sample-efficiency comparison, and the x-axis counts only environment steps. The VRER card also reports the mean age (current step minus collection step) of sampled transitions, averaged over VRER seeds, so the off-policyness of the current run is directly visible alongside ESS.

Configuration — strongly off-policy only. This demo is configured for the strongly off-policy regime — buffer capacity 50,000, reuse window 200,000 env steps, 8 replay updates per episode — because that is the regime where replay bias and gradient variance actually bind: replayed transitions come from substantially older, drifted policies and are reused many times. No results are claimed for any other regime. Task, reward, horizon H = 120, policy class, batch size (32), learning-rate default (0.005), and paired seeds (101, 202, 303) are as stated above. Estimator-internal hyperparameters: VRER snapshot refresh every 16 replay updates, IS clip [0.2, 5]; MSR K = 8, wmax = 5, classifier refit every 16 replay updates, FQE cadence as above.

  • Monte-Carlo estimated: everything in Scene 02 — returns, steps-to-threshold (median over seeds) — over 3 paired seeds (101, 202, 303), identical across estimators; per-seed returns are printed under the stat cards so the spread is checkable.
  • Gradient variance is measured on a common held-out batch: 128 transitions collected once per retrain from the shared initial policy, never trained on. For each agent, its own estimator's per-sample gradients (for VRER, the corrected difference w Â (∇logπθ − ∇logπθ̃); the constant μ̃ does not affect variance; for MSR, w̃(s)·(1/K)Σk Âψ ∇logπθ(ak|s) with ŵ self-normalised over the batch and fresh actions drawn from a fixed per-seed stream) are evaluated on that identical batch at the agent's current policy, and the reported number is the trace of their covariance, averaged over seeds. Re-benchmark — strongly off-policy (headless runs of this page's exact code, 31,200 env steps, batch 32, lr 0.005, seeds 101/202/303; mean return is the EMA reported on the cards; gradient variance is the common-batch trace at the final step). The configuration values below — including all MSR hyperparameters (K = 8, wmax = 5, FQE and classifier cadences) — were fixed from the design before benchmarking and were not adjusted after seeing results. Adding estimator (d) left (a)–(c) untouched: their numbers below reproduce the previously published strongly off-policy benchmark exactly (on-policy −96.3, naive −83.2, VRER −67.9), which checks that the harness change did not disturb them.

Strongly off-policy (buffer 50,000, window 200,000, 8 replay updates/episode; final mean sampled age ≈ 15,510 steps, VRER ESS ≈ 42% of batch, MSR ŵ ESS ≈ 37% at mean sampled age ≈ 15,360). Mean return at 31,200 steps: on-policy −96.3, naive replay −83.2, VRER −67.9, MSR-Replay −89.8 (per-seed −101.4 / −87.4 / −80.5 vs naive −96.4 / −64.9 / −88.2: MSR behind naive on two seeds, ahead on one). VRER was best on mean return and on every seed individually (−86.1 / −59.1 / −58.4). Steps to return ≥ −55: VRER reached it on 2 of 3 seeds (11,280 and 28,080); MSR's seed 303 crossed early (6,240) but did not hold it, ending at −80.5; naive replay and on-policy on none. Final gradient variance: on-policy 376, naive replay 695 on average but wildly seed-dependent (2,084 / 0.30 / 0.0003 across seeds), VRER 0.017, MSR 0.179. On-policy never touches the buffer, so its −96.3 is the configuration-independent reference point.

Honest reading: strongly off-policy is where the corrections are tested hardest, and the ranking here does not generalise beyond this regime. Naive replay's uncorrected bias compounds over older, more-reused data, while VRER leads on return as well as variance. MSR-Replay's variance prediction held and its return prediction did not. Its common-batch variance (0.179) is second only to VRER's and orders of magnitude below naive replay's 695 — with no importance ratios anywhere, as designed — and its diagnostics behave as intended (ŵ ESS ≈ 37% of batch, at the same sampled ages VRER faces). But on return MSR trails naive replay at this scale (−89.8 vs −83.2) and trails VRER by a wide margin. The design note predicted the off-policy contest would be decided by Qψ fit quality; the measurement says the FQE approximation-error floor — a small Q network refit on a 16-update cadence, queried at fresh actions off the behaviour support — costs more return here than the Bstate + Badv bias it removes, and MSR's mid-run trajectories were the most volatile of the four (one seed dipped to −254 at 15k steps before recovering to −87). MSR remains the only estimator of the four that attacks all three bias terms, and its bias no longer grows with buffer age — but at this task scale, with these fixed hyperparameters, that does not convert into a return win. VRER's trajectories were also volatile mid-run (one seed dipped to −130 at 15k steps before recovering to −58), so read the endpoints alongside the live curves rather than as a guarantee. The live run above reproduces this configuration; these numbers are the headless benchmark of the same code, not a separate tuned experiment.
